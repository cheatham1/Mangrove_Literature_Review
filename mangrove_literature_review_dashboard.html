Scoping review findings

Plastic pollution in mangrove ecosystems: impacts, mitigation actions, and policy responses

260 sources reviewed· 1993–2026· 7 thematic areas· 6 geographic regions
This scoping review synthesised 260 sources spanning 33 years (1993–2026) across peer-reviewed literature, grey literature, policy documents, and intergovernmental reports. The evidence base has grown rapidly: nearly half (48%) of all sources were published from 2023 onward, reflecting the emergence of mangrove plastic pollution as a distinct research field.

Evidence at a glance
Three key breakdowns of the 260-source corpus: literature type, geographic spread, and quality banding.
By literature type
Peer-reviewed articles make up two-thirds of the corpus (173), giving a strong academic base. Grey literature (47) and intergovernmental sources (20) supply most of the policy and institutional evidence; conference and other material is minimal.
By geographic region
“Global” (148, 57%) includes worldwide-scope work such as policy analyses, global reviews and datasets. Asia/Middle East leads region-specific fieldwork (59, 23%), while Africa, the Americas, Europe and the Pacific together make up under 20%.
By quality band
95% of sources score in the high band (>70% relevance) and only 2 fall below 40% — a well-curated corpus, so findings rest on consistently relevant sources.
Evidence by thematic area
Volume of evidence varies significantly across research themes. Plastic pollution state assessments have the strongest evidence base; mitigation practices and transboundary dynamics have the least.
Growth of the evidence base
Research output has accelerated sharply since 2023, driven by the Global Plastics Treaty negotiations and growing recognition of mangroves as pollutant sinks. Mitigation research is the most recent theme — 77% published since 2023. Breaking the yearly output down by theme shows what drove that growth: impact assessment surged around 2023, while policy and ecosystem-services research accelerated most in 2024–2026. Each bar's total height is the year's overall output.
How directly does the evidence target mangroves?
A core methodological question for this review: only a third of sources study plastics or POPs within mangroves directly. Nearly half draw on adjacent coastal and marine ecosystems as proxy evidence — important for context, but a step removed from mangrove-specific conditions.
By focus specificity
What this means for the evidence
Direct (86 sources, 33%) — plastics/POPs studied specifically in mangrove systems. The strongest, most on-target evidence, concentrated in plastic-pollution state assessments.

Mangrove-general (54, 21%) — mangrove ecology, services, or policy without a direct plastics focus. Provides essential ecological framing.

Adjacent ecosystems (120, 46%) — plastics/POPs in nearby coastal, marine, or soil systems, applied to mangroves by inference. Dominates impact, transboundary, and microplastics themes.
Direct evidence is heavily concentrated in plastic-pollution state assessments (61 of 86). Impact, transboundary, microplastics, and policy themes rely predominantly on adjacent-ecosystem proxy evidence.
Is the field moving toward direct mangrove study?
Focus specificity over time. Direct in-mangrove studies have grown in volume — from 10 before 2016 to 41 across 2023–26 — but their share of output has held near a third, because adjacent-ecosystem proxy work has grown just as fast. The corpus is expanding without becoming proportionally more mangrove-specific.

Assessing the quality and composition of the evidence base is essential for understanding how robust the review's findings are. This section examines literature types, quality scoring, and the intersection of research themes with geographic coverage.
Source types across thematic areas
Peer-reviewed evidence is strongest in plastic pollution research (84%) and transboundary research (83%), but weakest in policy analysis (16%), which relies heavily on intergovernmental and legal sources.
Quality score distribution
Sources were scored on focus relevance (0–10), literature type (3–5), keyword match (0–3), and geographic specificity (0–3), giving a maximum of 21.
Quality bands
95% of scored sources fell in the high band (>70%); the lowest scores sit at 38%, indicating a well-curated corpus with strong minimum relevance. All 260 sources are now scored.
Evidence quality by thematic area
Mean quality score per theme. Plastic-pollution state assessments score highest (96%), reflecting on-target, peer-reviewed fieldwork; policy and ecosystem-services evidence scores lowest (~72%), where grey literature and broad-scope studies dominate. Lower-scoring themes are where evidence is thinnest and least mangrove-specific.
Quality by source type
How each literature type distributes across the quality bands — bars show composition, with source counts in brackets. Peer-reviewed, grey, intergovernmental and policy sources are almost entirely high-band; conference and newsletter material skews medium, and the only low-scoring items are two map/database records with minimal focus relevance.
Quantity vs quality by theme
Each bubble is a theme: horizontal position is the number of sources, vertical position the mean quality score, and bubble size the share that is peer-reviewed. Plastic-pollution research is large, high-scoring and heavily peer-reviewed; policy is sizeable but lower-scoring and mostly non-peer-reviewed; mitigation is small but scores well.

Geographic evidence gaps

The field-work gap: direct vs proxy evidence by region
Raw source counts understate the geographic imbalance. Splitting each region by how directly it studies mangroves shows that outside Asia/Middle East, almost no direct on-mangrove field research exists — Europe has none, and the Pacific just one. Much regional evidence is adjacent-ecosystem or global-scope work applied by inference.
Asia/Middle East is the only region where direct mangrove field studies (38) outnumber proxy evidence. Global sources contribute 39 direct studies but are not tied to a specific site. Africa, the Americas, Europe, and the Pacific together account for just 9 direct on-mangrove studies.
